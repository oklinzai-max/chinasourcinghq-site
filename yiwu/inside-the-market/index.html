Yiwu: Inside the Market

An hour-by-hour picture of sourcing inside Yiwu International Trade City: how booths work, what to bring, how to collect quotes, and how to leave with usable supplier contacts.

Quick answer

A productive day in Yiwu is narrow, not broad: one district, one or two floors, 15–20 target booths, and a written record of every quote. Most first-time buyers over-walk and under-negotiate. Bring a notebook, a power bank, your passport, and a plan built the night before from the official market directory.

Start time
Most booths open in the morning — arrive at opening
Best window
Morning for quotes, afternoon for negotiation
Essential kit
Notebook, power bank, passport, business cards, samples bag
Records
Booth number + WeChat + written quote for every supplier

Who this guide is for: Buyers who have decided to visit Yiwu and want to know what the day actually feels like before they arrive.

The night before decides the day

The most valuable 40 minutes of a Yiwu trip happen in your hotel room the evening before.

Open the official Chinagoods directory, filter to your category, and write a list:

  • Shop number and district
  • Floor
  • Product lines that match what you buy
  • One question you want answered

Then plan the physical route. Districts are separate buildings; crossing between them costs time. Pick one district per half-day.

If you skip this step, you will default to walking the nearest aisle, which is rarely the aisle that matters.

08:30 — arrival and orientation

Arrive at opening. Booths are staffed, aisles are quiet, and you get the attention of the person who can actually quote.

At the entrance:

  • Note the district and entrance gate you used — useful when you need to leave quickly or meet a supplier.
  • Photograph the floor directory board. It is often more current than a printed map.
  • Set your phone to record location tags on photos, or make a habit of photographing booth numbers.

09:00–11:30 — the quote sweep

This is your broad pass. The goal is information, not commitment.

At each booth, ask four questions in this order:

  1. What is your minimum order quantity for this item? Per style, per colour, per carton.
  2. What is the unit price at that quantity? In RMB or USD, and whether it includes packaging.
  3. Do you have this in stock or is it made to order?
  4. Can I buy a sample today?

Write down the answers. Do not rely on memory — ten booths in, prices merge into a blur.

What to look at beyond the price:

  • Build quality at the seams, joints, hinges and printing.
  • Packaging — is it retail-ready or bulk? This affects landed cost significantly.
  • Consistency between the display sample and what they say they will ship.
  • Booth scale — a small booth with a narrow range is often a specialist; a large booth is often a trading company with wider range and less price flexibility.

11:30–13:00 — sample collection and admin

Buy samples while you are still in the building. A sample you did not buy is a sample you will not receive.

Practical points:

  • Expect to pay for samples unless you have an established relationship.
  • Ask whether the sample cost is deducted from a future order. Many suppliers agree.
  • Get a receipt or at least a WeChat message confirming what you paid for.
  • Keep samples in one bag with the booth card attached, not loose in a suitcase.

This is also the right time to send WeChat contact requests to every supplier you spoke to, with a one-line message: your name, country, product, and “we spoke at booth X today”.

13:00–14:00 — lunch, and a reset

Eat near the market. Leaving the district for a restaurant across town costs you an hour you do not get back.

Use lunch to review notes: which three or four booths deserve a second visit? Which quotes are suspiciously low? Which supplier answered the MOQ question vaguely?

14:00–16:30 — the negotiation pass

Return to your shortlist. This pass is about terms, not browsing.

Ask:

  • Price at a higher quantity. Even if you will not order that volume, it tells you where the margin sits.
  • Packaging options — polybag, colour box, printed carton, and the cost of each.
  • Lead time for a repeat order, not the first order.
  • Payment terms — deposit percentage, balance timing, accepted methods.
  • Certification — what documentation can they provide, and for which markets?
  • Whether they are the factory or a trading company. Both are legitimate, but the answer changes your follow-up.

If a supplier cannot answer a specification question clearly, that is information. Vagueness at the quotation stage tends to reappear at the quality-control stage.

16:30–17:30 — close the loop

Before you leave the market:

  • Photograph every sample next to its booth card.
  • Send each shortlisted supplier a written summary of what you discussed, in simple English, and ask them to confirm.
  • Note the booth’s closing day for the rest of your trip.

A supplier who replies within a day is telling you something about how they will handle an order.

What the market is not

  • It is not a factory. Booths are showrooms; production happens elsewhere. If a factory visit matters, ask for it explicitly.
  • It is not a fixed-price environment. Quotes vary widely for identical specifications.
  • It is not a place for deep technical due diligence. For regulated products, the market is a starting point, not a compliance check.

Physical survival notes

  • Wear comfortable shoes. Concrete floors, tens of thousands of steps.
  • Carry a power bank. Maps, translation and WeChat drain battery fast.
  • Bring water. Vending machines exist, but queues at peak times do not.
  • Keep your passport with you. Hotels register guests, and some services require ID.
  • Plan a lighter second day. Decision fatigue is real; the third day of a first trip is usually the least productive.

Turning a market day into an order

A market visit produces contacts, not a purchase. The follow-up is where the order happens:

  1. Send a specification sheet with your exact requirements.
  2. Request a formal quotation with Incoterm, lead time and payment terms.
  3. Compare at least three suppliers in a supplier comparison sheet.
  4. Order a paid sample and test it before committing.
  5. Calculate landed cost with the cost calculator.

Frequently asked questions

Can I visit without an appointment?

Yes. The market is open to buyers and most booths accept walk-ins. Appointments help for larger booths and for factory visits, but they are not required for a first visit.

How many booths can I realistically visit in a day?

Fifteen to twenty meaningful conversations in a day is a good result. Beyond that, notes blur and quotes get confused. Depth beats volume.

Do I need to speak Chinese?

No, but it helps. Many booths use translation apps, and written specifications reduce misunderstandings. Learn a few phrases for numbers and quantities.

Is it acceptable to take photos?

Usually yes for products you are considering, and it is polite to ask. Photograph the booth number in the same frame — this is the single most useful habit in the market.

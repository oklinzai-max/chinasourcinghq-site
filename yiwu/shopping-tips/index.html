Practical buying advice for Yiwu International Trade City: how to ask about MOQ, negotiate without damaging the relationship, order samples, check quality and avoid the most common first-trip mistakes.

Quick answer

Ask for MOQ per colour before asking for price, compare at least three booths for the same specification, treat every quote as an opening position, pay for samples and test them, and confirm packaging and carton details in writing. The cheapest quote is rarely the lowest landed cost.

Ask first
MOQ per style and per colour
Compare
At least 3 quotes for the same spec
Sample
Paid, tested, and approved in writing
Confirm
Packaging, carton size, lead time, payment terms

Who this guide is for: Buyers who have a market plan and want to negotiate and evaluate suppliers without wasting the trip.

Start with the question that changes the price

Most buyers ask “how much?” first. That is the wrong order.

Ask instead:

  1. What is your MOQ for this item — per style, per colour, per carton?
  2. What is the unit price at that quantity, and does it include packaging?
  3. What is the price at three times that quantity?

The third question reveals the shape of the supplier’s cost structure. A small drop suggests a trading company with limited room. A large drop suggests a factory with fixed setup costs and volume incentives.

Only then discuss your target price.

Negotiation that keeps the relationship

Aggressive haggling is a bad strategy in a market where you may need the same supplier twice.

Better approaches:

  • Trade volume for price. “If we consolidate three items with you, what happens to the price?”
  • Trade commitment for terms. “If we place a repeat order within 90 days, can the sample cost be credited?”
  • Trade simplicity for price. “If we accept your standard packaging, is there a better price?”
  • Ask what is flexible. Suppliers will often move on packaging, payment timing or freight terms before they move on unit price.

What not to do:

  • Invent a competitor’s price. Suppliers in the same district know each other’s ranges.
  • Negotiate for an hour, then buy nothing. It costs you goodwill you may need later.
  • Push below a credible floor. If a price looks impossible, either the specification has changed or the material has.

Samples: the cheapest insurance you can buy

Order samples for anything you intend to reorder. Then:

  • Test the sample against your actual use case, not just visually.
  • Keep one sample sealed as the reference for the bulk order.
  • Photograph the sample with the booth card and the quote in one frame.
  • Ask whether the sample cost is credited against a future order.
  • Record the sample in a tracking sheet — the sample tracking template exists for exactly this.

A sample that arrives in three weeks and sits unopened on a desk has taught you nothing.

Quality checks you can do at the booth

You cannot audit a factory from a showroom, but you can catch obvious problems:

  • Consistency: ask for three units of the same item and compare them.
  • Finishing: seams, mould lines, sharp edges, print alignment, smell.
  • Packaging: will it survive international shipping? Is the retail box crushed easily?
  • Labels and markings: are certifications printed, or claimed?
  • Function: test hinges, zips, switches, batteries, seals.

For regulated products — electronics, children’s items, food contact materials, cosmetics — a booth conversation is not compliance evidence. Ask for test reports and verify the standards required by your destination market.

Packaging and carton details decide your landed cost

Two suppliers quoting the same unit price can differ by 20% in landed cost because of packaging.

Ask for:

  • Unit packaging type and dimensions
  • Units per inner box and per carton
  • Carton dimensions and gross weight
  • Carton strength and stacking suitability
  • Whether retail-ready packaging is available, and at what cost

Then calculate. The sourcing cost calculator lets you add freight, duty and inspection costs to see the real per-unit figure.

Payment and paperwork

  • Get a written quotation with specification, quantity, unit price, Incoterm, lead time and payment terms. A WeChat message counts if it is specific.
  • Pay production deposits to a company account, not a personal one, unless you have a long relationship.
  • Keep a simple purchase record — date, supplier, item, quantity, amount, expected ship date.
  • Confirm what documents the supplier provides: invoice, packing list, and any certificate of origin or test report.

If a supplier resists putting terms in writing, treat that as a signal rather than an inconvenience.

Judging export experience in five minutes

You do not need a credit check to tell an export-ready booth from a domestic wholesaler. Five questions do most of the work:

  1. “Which countries do you ship to, and what documents do you issue?” An exporter answers with countries and document names: commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, test report.
  2. “Does this certificate cover this exact model?” Ask to see the report, not the badge on the wall. Model numbers must match.
  3. “Which Incoterm do you quote most often?” If the answer is a blank look, you are probably talking to a domestic seller.
  4. “What happens if the goods arrive with a defect rate above [your threshold]?” A supplier with export experience has a procedure. A domestic seller promises it will not happen.
  5. Look at the packaging behind the counter. Export cartons with shipping marks tell you more than a catalogue.

None of this is proof of quality. It tells you whether the booth has done this before — which is the difference between a supplier who can follow your instructions and one who will improvise.

Customisation: what can actually be changed

Yiwu is very good at decorating an existing product and much weaker at engineering a new one. Being clear about which you need saves weeks.

Change Usually possible? What to ask
Your logo printed or engraved Yes, at moderate MOQ per colour Printing method, position, setup or plate cost, MOQ per design
Colour change Usually, subject to a dye lot minimum Minimum per colour, whether a colour sample is available, colour-fastness
Custom packaging — printed box, bag, insert, label Yes, and this is where most buyers add value MOQ per packaging design, whether packaging is packed at the booth or at the factory
Material or specification change Sometimes, if the factory already runs that material Whether the change affects certification, weight or carton size
Size change Depends on tooling; often limited to cutting or assembly changes Whether a new mould is needed and who pays for it
A genuinely new product or mechanism Rarely in the market itself Ask for a factory introduction, or move to the industrial cluster — Shenzhen, Zhongshan or Foshan — where engineering is the business

Always ask what stays the same as well as what changes. A supplier who lists the parts they cannot touch is usually the one who understands the product.

When English runs out

Most booths in the larger districts handle basic English. The failures happen in the details — colours, tolerances, packaging, deadlines — which is exactly where misunderstandings cost money.

  • Write a numbered requirement list with photos, dimensions and quantities. Hand it over rather than explaining it.
  • Use a translation app for conversation, but confirm in writing. Machine translation is fine for “can you make this in blue” and dangerous for “the tolerance is ±0.5mm”.
  • Ask for the quotation in writing, with the Chinese product name next to the English one. You will need the Chinese name if you order again.
  • Never rely on a spoken agreement about price. A photo of a handwritten quote with the booth number is enough.
  • If you hire an interpreter, agree the fee, the hours and the scope before you start, and have them read the quotation back to you rather than summarise it.

After you buy: getting it out of the market

Most booths quote ex-works or FOB, which means the goods still have to reach a port or a courier. Three routes are common: the supplier ships via their own forwarder, you use a forwarder you already work with, or the market’s own logistics desks consolidate small orders. Ask which one the supplier assumes before you agree a price — a quote that excludes inland transport is not comparable with one that includes it.

The samples, shipping and logistics guide explains consolidation, Incoterms and what documents to expect.

Red flags worth taking seriously

  • A price far below every other quote for an identical specification.
  • Refusal to provide a sample, or a sample that differs from the display item.
  • Vague answers about lead time.
  • Pressure to pay a large deposit immediately.
  • No company name on the booth card or quotation.
  • Unwillingness to state whether they are a factory or a trading company.

None of these is automatically disqualifying. Together, they are a reason to slow down.

Cultural and practical notes

  • Relationships matter more than one transaction. A short, polite follow-up beats a pushy close.
  • Direct “no” is often softened. Listen for “we will check” and “it may be difficult” as signals rather than commitments.
  • Business cards still matter. Bring them; they make you memorable.
  • WeChat is the working channel. Set it up before you arrive and use it consistently.
  • Lunch is a real relationship tool. Accepting a simple meal with a supplier is normal and useful.

What to do the day after

  1. Send each shortlisted supplier a written summary and ask for confirmation.
  2. Build the comparison in the supplier comparison spreadsheet.
  3. Request formal quotations with Incoterms.
  4. Decide which two suppliers deserve a factory visit or a paid sample order.
  5. Follow up within 48 hours — responsiveness is a useful quality signal.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get my own logo on products bought in Yiwu?

Usually yes — printing, engraving and custom packaging are routine, each with its own minimum order per design. What is harder is changing the product itself: a new mould or a new mechanism is factory work, not market work. Ask for the setup or tooling cost and the MOQ per design before you commit.

Do Yiwu sellers speak English?

Larger booths usually handle basic English; smaller ones often rely on translation apps. Write your requirements as a numbered list with photos and dimensions, and confirm every quotation in writing, with the Chinese product name alongside the English one.

How much can I negotiate in Yiwu?

There is no fixed answer, and anyone quoting a percentage is guessing. What matters is leverage: quantity, repeat orders, mixed-order size and whether you are talking to the decision maker. Ask for the price at a higher quantity and see how the number moves.

Should I pay a deposit at the booth?

For a sample, paying at the booth is normal. For a production order, prefer a transfer to the company account with a written specification, and avoid paying a personal account unless you have an established relationship.

What if the sample is good but the bulk order is not?

That is the classic risk. Protect yourself with a written specification, a retained approved sample, and a pre-shipment inspection for anything above a modest order value.

Do suppliers accept PayPal or credit cards?

Some do, many do not. Bank transfer and Chinese mobile payment are more common. Confirm before you commit, and account for transfer fees and exchange costs in your landed cost.

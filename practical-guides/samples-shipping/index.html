Samples, Shipping & Logistics

How samples and production orders move from China to your market: courier versus freight forwarder, consolidation, Incoterms explained, customs duties, and what to confirm before anything ships.

Quick answer

Use an express courier for samples and small trial orders, and a freight forwarder for anything pallet or container sized. Confirm the Incoterm, who clears customs, who pays duty, and how the goods are packed — before production, not before shipping.

Samples
Express courier is usually simplest
Bulk
Freight forwarder, by air or sea
Incoterm
Decides where your responsibility starts
Duty
Set by your country's customs, not the supplier

Who this guide is for: Buyers moving from quotations to physical goods, especially first-time importers.

Sample first, then decide

A sample is not a formality. It is the only reliable way to check whether the product matches the specification and whether the supplier can execute consistently.

Ordering samples well:

  • Ask for a paid sample of the exact specification you intend to order.
  • Get a dispatch date, not an arrival estimate.
  • Ask whether the sample cost is credited against a production order.
  • Record it in the sample tracking sheet.
  • Test it properly, then keep one unit sealed as the approved reference.

A sample that arrives and sits unopened has cost you money and taught you nothing.

Courier or forwarder?

Situation Best option Why
One or two samples Express courier Door to door, simple clearance, fast
Small trial order (a few cartons) Express courier or air freight Balance of speed and cost
Pallet-sized order Air freight via forwarder Better rate than express at volume
Container order Sea freight via forwarder Lowest cost per unit, slowest
Multiple suppliers Consolidated shipment One clearance, lower cost

The mistake to avoid is assuming sea freight is always cheaper. It has fixed costs — documentation, terminal handling, customs brokerage, inland delivery — that can exceed the saving on a small shipment.

Incoterms, in plain language

Incoterms define where your cost and risk begin. Four you will meet constantly:

  • EXW (Ex Works) — the goods are yours at the supplier’s door. You arrange everything, including export clearance in China. Cheapest on paper, most work in practice.
  • FOB (Free On Board) — the supplier delivers to the port and clears export. You arrange and pay the main freight and import. The most common term for Chinese exports.
  • CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) — the supplier pays freight and insurance to your port. You still handle import clearance and duty.
  • DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) — the supplier delivers to your door with duty paid. Convenient, but you have less visibility and sometimes less control over how duty is declared.

Two rules:

  1. Never compare prices across different Incoterms. FOB and DDP quotes are not the same product.
  2. Write the Incoterm into the purchase order, with the named port or place.

Customs, duty and the importer of record

Your country’s customs rules decide duty, not the supplier. To estimate your landed cost you need:

  • HS classification for your product
  • Duty rate for that classification and origin
  • Import VAT or sales tax treatment
  • Any anti-dumping, quota or licensing rules
  • Conformity requirements — CE, UKCA, FCC, FDA, and so on

We do not publish duty rates, because they are specific to your country, product and date. Enter your own figure in the sourcing cost calculator, and confirm it with a customs broker or your national customs authority.

A supplier offering “no duty” or “gift declaration” to reduce charges is proposing an inaccurate declaration. The risk sits with the importer.

Packaging and damage

For anything fragile or bulky — lighting, ceramics, furniture — packaging is a cost driver and a risk control:

  • Specify carton grade and protection in the purchase order.
  • Get carton dimensions and gross weight before booking freight.
  • Understand container loading — how many units fit, and whether weight limits bind first.
  • Insure the shipment and know the claims process.
  • Agree the remedy for damage before shipping.

Batteries and restricted goods

Lithium batteries, liquids, magnets, aerosols and some cosmetics face transport restrictions. Express couriers and airlines apply different rules. Declare accurately — an undeclared battery can cause a shipment to be refused or destroyed, at your cost.

Documents you should receive

  • Commercial invoice
  • Packing list
  • Bill of lading or air waybill
  • Certificate of origin, where applicable
  • Test reports or conformity documents, where applicable

Check that the invoice description, quantity and value match the shipment. Mismatches create customs delays that can cost more than the goods’ value in time.

A simple shipping checklist

  • Sample approved and reference kept
  • Purchase order states specification, quantity, Incoterm, packing
  • Carton dimensions and weights received
  • Freight quote obtained for your actual volume
  • Duty rate confirmed from an official source
  • Insurance decision made
  • Documents received and checked before departure
  • Tracking recorded in your sample/order sheet

Common mistakes

  • Comparing an FOB price with a DDP price.
  • Forgetting import VAT when calculating margin.
  • Shipping a container before checking packaging.
  • Using express courier for a pallet and paying far more than air freight.
  • Assuming the supplier handles import clearance under CIF.
  • Not insuring a fragile shipment.
  • Declaring a lower value to reduce duty — the liability is yours.

Frequently asked questions

Who pays customs duty?

Whoever is the importer of record under the shipment terms. With DDP the seller arranges it; with FOB or CIF you usually do. Duty is set by your country, not by the supplier, so a supplier promising 'no duty' is usually describing a workaround you should examine carefully.

How long do samples take?

Express courier is typically a few business days after dispatch, but production of the sample and customs clearance can add time. Ask for a dispatch date, not an arrival promise.

Can I consolidate orders from several suppliers?

Yes, and it often saves money. A forwarder can receive goods at a warehouse in China, combine them, and ship as one consignment. It adds handling time and requires clear labelling per supplier.

Is sea freight always cheaper?

Per kilogram, usually. Per shipment, only if you have enough volume to justify the fixed costs and can accept the transit time. Small orders are often cheaper by air express despite the higher rate.

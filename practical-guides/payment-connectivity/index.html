Payment & Connectivity in China

How international buyers handle money and mobile data in China: linking foreign cards to mobile payment apps, cash and ATMs, eSIM options, and which services may not work on local networks.

Quick answer

Set up mobile payment before you fly — Alipay or WeChat Pay with an international card — and carry a small amount of cash as backup. For data, choose between a travel eSIM and a local SIM. Check which apps you depend on, because several international services are not directly accessible on mainland networks.

Primary payment
Mobile payment apps linked to an international card
Backup
Cash in RMB, plus a second card
Data options
Travel eSIM, local SIM, international roaming
Reality check
Some international apps and services do not work on mainland networks

Who this guide is for: First-time visitors to China who need working payments and data from the moment they land.

The one thing to set up before you fly

Mobile payment. In practice, China runs on QR codes, and a foreign card linked to Alipay or WeChat Pay turns your phone into a working wallet for most daily transactions.

Do this at home, not at the airport:

  1. Install the app.
  2. Register with your phone number.
  3. Complete identity verification with your passport.
  4. Link your international card.
  5. Test a small transaction if possible.

Verification can take time and occasionally requires a second attempt. Doing it while tired, on airport Wi-Fi, is a bad plan.

Where the card-linking menu lives

Menu names change between app versions, so treat these as the path rather than the exact wording:

  • Alipay: Me → Bank Cards → Add Bank Card. Where an “international card” option appears, use it.
  • WeChat: Me → Services → Wallet → Bank Cards → Add a Card.

Both apps may ask for passport details and a short selfie as part of verification. Complete this at home, on a stable connection, with the passport photo page flat and well lit.

If your card is rejected

Symptom Likely cause What to do
Declined at the linking step Your bank blocks mainland China transactions by default Call the issuer and ask them to allow China transactions
Name mismatch error Card name and passport name differ (middle names, spacing) Enter the name exactly as printed on the passport
Verification loops back to the start Photo quality or unstable network Retry on Wi-Fi, in daylight, with the passport flat
Linking works but a payment fails That merchant does not accept foreign-card funding Try another merchant — not every QR code accepts international cards
Only small amounts go through Per-transaction or annual limits apply to foreign cards Check the current limits inside the app; they change

Two habits that prevent a bad first day:

  • Link two cards from different issuers. One being blocked should not end your trip.
  • Carry ¥300–500 in cash until you have completed one successful QR payment. After that, cash is backup rather than necessity.

What mobile payment covers

  • Taxis and ride-hailing
  • Metro and bus tickets via QR codes
  • Markets, convenience stores and most restaurants
  • Many hotels, though large hotels also accept cards
  • Some ticket machines for attractions and transport

Fees and limits for foreign cards have changed repeatedly. Check the current terms inside the app rather than relying on a blog figure — including this one.

Cash: still useful, no longer essential

Carry a modest amount of RMB for:

  • Small vendors who prefer cash
  • A backup if your phone dies
  • Tips or incidentals where QR codes are inconvenient

Withdraw from bank ATMs with a card that has no foreign transaction fee where possible. Airport exchange counters are convenient but usually poor value.

Cards

Physical cards work at:

  • International hotels
  • Larger retailers
  • Some restaurants in major cities

They fail at:

  • Market stalls
  • Small shops
  • Many taxis
  • Some ticket machines

Carry a second card from a different network, and tell your bank you are travelling. A blocked card in a wholesale market is an avoidable crisis.

Connectivity: three options

Option Setup Best for Trade-offs
Travel eSIM Install before departure Short trips, arriving connected Data-only in many cases; no local number
Local SIM Buy on arrival with passport Longer stays, local calls Registration time, phone number needed
International roaming Activate with your home operator Very short trips, simplicity Cost, and the same network restrictions apply

Travel eSIM

Install and activate before you fly, so you land with data. Check:

  • Whether it is data-only or includes a number
  • Which networks it uses
  • Whether it routes traffic inside or outside the mainland — this affects which apps work
  • Whether hotspot/tethering is allowed

A data eSIM will not give you a Chinese phone number, which matters for some app registrations and for suppliers who prefer to call.

Local SIM

Requires passport registration at an official operator store. Gives you a local number, which helps with:

  • WeChat verification
  • Delivery and courier contact
  • Suppliers calling you

Allow 30–60 minutes in a store, and bring your passport.

International roaming

Simplest, usually most expensive. Useful for a two-day trip where you barely leave the hotel.

Which apps will not work

Several international services are not directly accessible on mainland networks. This affects:

  • Some Google services, including Google Maps and Gmail
  • Several social platforms
  • Some cloud and collaboration tools

Plan around it:

  • Download offline maps before you arrive, or use a Chinese map app.
  • Tell colleagues which channel will work — WeChat is the practical default in China.
  • Keep critical documents offline in case cloud access fails.
  • Check your company’s IT policy on connectivity tools before you travel. Use of network circumvention services is regulated in China.

We do not provide instructions for bypassing network restrictions. Plan for the environment as it is.

WeChat: install it, use it

WeChat is not optional for sourcing. Suppliers, hotels and drivers use it. Set up:

  • An account verified with your phone number
  • Your real name and company in the profile
  • The translation feature, which is adequate for basic messages
  • A habit of sending written summaries after meetings

See business communication for how to use it effectively with suppliers.

Practical setup checklist

  • Mobile payment app installed and card linked
  • Passport verification completed
  • Second card and some cash as backup
  • Bank notified of travel
  • eSIM installed and tested, or SIM plan decided
  • Offline maps downloaded
  • WeChat working before departure
  • Power bank packed (and within airline limits)

Common mistakes

  • Relying on a physical card at a market.
  • Arriving with no data and no offline map.
  • Leaving payment verification until arrival.
  • Assuming your usual work apps will behave normally.
  • Carrying only large notes when small vendors cannot make change.
  • Not testing the eSIM before flying, then discovering it needs manual configuration.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use my Visa or Mastercard in China?

At large hotels, some restaurants and international retailers, often yes. At markets, small shops and taxis, acceptance is unreliable. Link your card to a mobile payment app instead of relying on the physical card.

Do I need a Chinese bank account?

No, for a sourcing trip. International cards can be linked to the main mobile payment apps, subject to verification and any current limits.

Will my usual apps work in China?

Several major international services are not directly accessible on mainland networks, including some Google services and social platforms. Check what you rely on for work and plan an alternative before you travel.

Is Wi-Fi reliable in hotels?

Generally yes in international hotels, though speeds vary and some sites remain inaccessible. Do not rely on hotel Wi-Fi for critical calls without testing.
